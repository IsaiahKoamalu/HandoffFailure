Teaching the Ship to Land

A PPO agent trained against the real flight model, wired into the real cockpit. It works end to end — and it still loses to ten lines of proportional control. This is the log of how that happened and what each failure actually taught.

Read this before the graphs

PPO never beat the baseline, and fine-tuning actively destroyed a working cloned policy — while its own training reward went up. That gap between "the reward improved" and "the ship landed" is the single most useful thing in this document, and it is a reward-design failure, not a library bug.

The shipped policy is the behaviour clone at 39.7%. Inside the actual game it lands 8 out of 8 attempts — but that number is flattered, and the reason is worth knowing: the game has terrain colliders and the ship's own auto-levelling, and the training environment has neither. The 39.7% is the harsher, more honest measure.

Rig

Two processes in lock-step

Godot owns the physics; PyTorch owns the gradients. They exchange one line of JSON per physics tick over loopback. The read blocks inside _physics_process, which stalls the whole engine until Python answers — that is the point. Nothing is dropped, nothing races, and a rollout replays exactly.

The agent writes to Ship.command_thrust and Ship.command_turn — the same seam a mouse and keyboard feed. Everything downstream is the shipping code. That was a deliberate constraint: a policy trained against a simplified copy of the ship is a policy that cannot fly the real one, and you find that out last.

Log

Nine things that were wrong

In order, because each one was only visible once the previous had been fixed. Six are reward or action-space design; two are ordinary engineering; one is a fact about the ship that made the original plan impossible.

  1. The ship cannot land without flight assist

    Thrust is 10.0 units/s². Surface gravity is 9.81. That is a thrust-to-weight ratio of 1.02 — a ship pointing straight up at full power decelerates at 0.19 units/s². Killing 30 units/s of descent would take 158 seconds and 2,400 units of altitude, more than a planet radius.

    The original plan was to train with assist off, on the theory that the agent should learn to replace it. There was no policy to find: the task had no solution. The assist's drag term is this ship's actual landing system, capping free fall near g / 1.6 = 6.1 units/s. It stays on, and the policy's job is to brake from there to under 2.0.

    TWR = 10.00 / 9.81 = 1.02 net decel = 0.19 u/s^2 to arrest 30 u/s: 158 s, ~2400 units (planet radius = 180)

  2. Shaping scaled by the wrong speed

    Observations are normalised by orbital speed at the surface — about 42 units/s. Reusing that scale in the reward made the entire difference between a 6 units/s crash and a perfect touchdown worth about 0.7 reward, against a 100-point landing bonus. The policy correctly ignored speed.

    Rescaling to a landing-relevant 10 units/s was the first change that moved the landing rate at all. A normalisation constant that is right for the network's input can be badly wrong for the objective.

  3. A pass/fail bonus is a cliff

    The first reward paid +100 for a clean touchdown and nothing otherwise. Every near miss scored identically to a forty-units-per-second smash, so there was no gradient pointing back toward a landing — only a plateau with a hole in it.

    Scoring speed and alignment continuously turned "not quite a landing" into a slope. The strict pass/fail test survives, but only to label outcomes in the logs.

  4. Penalising crashes taught it to refuse to land

    With an impact-scaled penalty, hovering for a full episode cost about −1.8 while a bad landing cost −3. The policy learned to stall just off the deck until the clock ran out — which was, given that reward, the correct answer.

    timeout rate: 24.9% -> 40.6% -> 80.0% -> 87.5% (iterations 20-60)

    The penalty is now flat and small, and time is charged at a rate that makes running out the clock worse than any touchdown at all.

  5. Alignment was gated but barely shaped

    Belly-down alignment gates the terminal bonus outright. At a shaping weight of 0.3 it was worth at most 3 reward across a whole episode, against 120 for landing. The policy learned to arrive fast and roughly sideways: return around 30, clean landings around 3%.

    Raising the weight to 1.5 roughly doubled the landing rate inside 400k steps. Shape a gate in proportion to the gate.

  6. Thrust was direction-only, so noise replaced the command

    Ship._physics_process thrusts along movement.normalized(). A keyboard has no analogue axis, so the player's thrust was always all-or-nothing — fine for a person. For a policy exploring with Gaussian noise it is poison: when the mean command is near zero, the noise does not perturb the direction, it is the direction, and the ship fires at full power at random.

    The first fix was a deadzone, so "no thrust" could be expressed at all. That created problem seven.

  7. The deadzone made the mean policy a different policy

    Training samples actions; play takes the distribution mean. With a deadzone, a mean action can sit inside the dead region while sampled actions routinely escape it — so the policy learned to rely on its own exploration noise to produce thrust, and taking the mean removed the thrust entirely.

    behaviour clone, evaluated at the mean 39.7% landed after 3.0M steps of PPO fine-tune 3.0% landed training return over the same window rose to ~50

    Replacing the discontinuity with continuous thrust authority — command length scales the force — made the mean representative again. This is the bug I would most expect to ship unnoticed: every training metric looked fine.

  8. A cloned actor with a random critic

    Behaviour cloning arrives with a competent actor and an untrained critic. Its advantage estimates are noise, and the first PPO updates spend that noise dismantling the policy. Landings fell from 36% to 2.5% over 150 iterations, with the training return falling alongside them.

    Twenty-five iterations of value-only updates before the policy is allowed to move made the fine-tune stop being a demolition. It still did not make it an improvement — see the verdict below.

  9. The autopilot waited for an altitude the ship cannot reach

    The training environment ends an episode at altitude zero, measured to the ship's origin, because there are no colliders in training. In the game the hull has landing legs, so a ship resting on the ground reads about 1.4.

    The in-game autopilot waited for zero. It flew the descent perfectly, eight for eight, and then hovered a metre and a half off the deck until it timed out. No test that lived inside the training environment could have caught this.

    before: 8/8 "disengaged (timed out) at altitude 1.4-1.9" after: 8/8 "landed speed 0.40-1.38 align 0.960-1.000"

The diagnosis

Mean episode length falls from 769 ticks to 446 across the fine-tune while landings fall with it and return stays flat. The policy is buying time savings with impact speed, and the reward says that is correct: at −0.02 per tick, arriving 300 ticks sooner is worth 6 reward, and the graded bonus does not punish a moderately hard landing by much more than that. The objective and the metric came apart, and the objective won.

Ship

It runs in the game

The network is not a demo sitting beside the project. Get in the ship, get near a planet, press P. rl/neural_pilot.gd loads policy.json and runs the forward pass in GDScript — four small matrix-vector products, no ONNX runtime, no GDExtension, nothing to install.

Two guards make that claim checkable rather than hopeful. The observation has exactly one definition, LandingEnv.build_observation, used by both the trainer and the cockpit; if they had drifted the policy would still run, still look busy, and quietly fly a different task. And the GDScript forward pass is diffed against the numpy one on fixed probes:

probe 0 gdscript [ 0.00768 0.32829 -0.05093 -0.25997 0.01019 -0.19487] numpy [ 0.00768 0.32829 -0.05093 -0.25997 0.01019 -0.19487] largest disagreement: 4.79e-07 (tolerance 2e-04) PASS

And the end-to-end check flies main.tscn itself — the real Ship node, the real autopilot, real terrain colliders — for eight randomised approaches: 8/8 landed, touching down between 0.40 and 1.38 units/s with alignment at 0.96 to 1.000.

Next

What I would change

The honest summary is that the environment, the bridge and the deployment path are solid, and the reward is not. Everything below follows from that.

  • Optimise the thing being measured. Drop the per-tick time charge, or make the terminal bonus dominate it by an order of magnitude. The current objective genuinely prefers a fast hard landing to a slow soft one.
  • Score alignment against the terrain normal, not the radial. On Planet 2 the relief is 42% of the radius and 22% of the surface is steeper than the ship's own floor_max_angle. "Belly toward the planet centre" is the wrong target on a slope, and it adds noise to every episode.
  • Give the critic a real warm start — regress it on Monte-Carlo returns from the demonstration rollout rather than freezing the actor and hoping.
  • Anneal the exploration noise explicitly. With the entropy bonus at 0.003 and no floor on the standard deviation, the sampled and mean policies drift apart late in training with nothing reporting it.
  • Terminate on the collider, not on altitude zero. Streaming one terrain patch per agent is expensive, but a cheap capsule test against the analytic surface at the hull's actual clearance would close the training/gameplay gap that produced failure nine.

The larger lesson is the ordinary one, and it is worth more than a working policy would have been: on a task where a competent controller fits in ten lines, reinforcement learning has to earn its place, and here it has not yet. The thing that made it legible that it had not was keeping a hand-written baseline in the evaluation harness from the start.
