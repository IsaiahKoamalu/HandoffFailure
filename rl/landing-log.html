Teaching the Ship to Land

A PPO rig wired into the real flight model and the real cockpit. The policy now lands 97% of the time — and the 62% this page used to report was measuring the ship falling in the sea. This is the log, including the retraction.

Correction

The metric was measuring splashdowns

This page previously reported a result that was wrong

An earlier version of this document said the hand-written baseline landed 62.3% of the time and the behaviour clone 39.7%, and drew conclusions from the gap. Both figures were counting ships falling into the ocean as successful landings. The environment had no notion of water. Every number in that comparison has been remeasured and is superseded by the scoreboard below.

Three things had to line up for this to go unnoticed, and each was individually reasonable.

The planets were almost entirely ocean. Land covered 2.7%, 9.8% and 4.5% of the three bodies. Agents were placed uniformly on the sphere, so essentially every episode ended at sea.

Water is soft. Ship._water_drag is 3.0 against the flight assist's 1.6, and buoyancy is on top of that. A ship hitting the ocean at terminal velocity is braked well below the 2.0 units/s touchdown gate long before it reaches the seabed.

The success test was assembled from what the environment happened to expose. It asked for speed ≤ 2.0 and align ≥ 0.9 at altitude zero — both true of a gentle splashdown. Nothing in it asked where the ship had come down, because nothing in the environment could answer that.

original controller, original planets, classified after the fact: ditched 85.6% (196 / 229 episodes) crashed 14.4% ( 33 / 229 episodes) landed 0.0%

Re-run today against the same old planets with the current outcome classifier, the controller that "landed 62.3% of the time" puts 85.6% of its episodes in the water and lands on dry ground exactly never.

The transferable part

The failure was not about water. It was that a success metric built only from conditions the environment already exposed will quietly answer a narrower question than the one intended — and while it does, everything downstream looks healthy. The training curves were fine. The evaluation harness was fine. The baseline comparison was fine. All of them were answering "did it arrive gently?" when the question was "did it land?"

Read this before the graphs

Fine-tuning the clone with PPO takes it from 97.0% to 2.5%. Unlike the earlier round, this is not the objective and the metric disagreeing — the training return falls too, from 75 to 21. PPO is dismantling a good initialisation and failing to recover, which points at the fine-tuning setup rather than at the reward. The shipped policy is the clone.

Plant

The ship could not stop

Underneath the metric bug was a physical one. Thrust was 10.0 units/s² against a surface gravity of 9.81 — a thrust-to-weight ratio of 1.02. That is tight enough to be interesting, and it interacts badly with one line of the flight assist:

// _process_flight_assist if movement != Vector3.ZERO: # Leave the axis being thrust along alone; only trim the rest. var wanted := movement.normalized() drift -= wanted * drift.dot(wanted)

That is correct for a pilot — the assist should not fight a deliberate burn. But it means a ship braking its own descent gets thrust minus gravity and nothing else: 0.19 units/s². Shedding a six-units-a-second fall takes 31 seconds and ninety units of altitude, and the ship never spawns that high. A controlled touchdown on land was not difficult, it was unreachable. The only reason anything appeared to land was the ocean doing the braking.

Thrust is now 18.0, a ratio of about 1.83. Flight assist still does real work — it caps free fall near g / 1.6 = 6.1 units/s, three times the touchdown gate — but the ship is no longer relying on it to stop.

Then the controller had to be retuned

Raising thrust alone changed nothing: the baseline still landed 0% of the time, now arriving at 3.11 instead of 4.37 units/s. Its proportional gain was 15, so a three-units-a-second overshoot asked for barely half thrust — which was academic when there was no thrust to give, and was pure unused authority afterwards.

gain 15, taper 0.20R 0.0% landed 3.11 u/s gain 45, taper 0.15R 99.3% landed 1.04 u/s

Raising the gain to 45 and tightening the flare took it from 0% to 99.3% with no other change. Retune the controller when you change the plant — obvious in hindsight, and it cost an hour of suspecting the environment.

Terrain

A hundred continents is not terrain

The planets also needed fixing, and for a reason that reads as a units bug. continent_frequency is sampled on the unit sphere, so it means features per planet — around 1.0 gives a handful of continents. Two of the three planets were set to 100.0, asking for a hundred continents across one sphere. The result is not terrain, it is static, and it made the surface unlandable.

"Too spiky" is an impression, so rl/terrain_report.tscn turns it into a number: ground slope in degrees over an eight-unit baseline, which is roughly a ship length and exactly the scale that decides whether landing struts can sit level.

Total land area cannot tell one continent from ten thousand islands, and that difference is the whole of whether a world looks like a world.

Task

Finding land, not just ground

With water now a failure, the agent needs to be able to see it. Four observations were added, bringing the vector to 18. Without them the task is not merely hard, it is unobservable: an ocean and a plain are identical in every other channel, so no policy over them could beat a coin flip on where to come down.

The signal is the continent mask itself — the same scalar get_terrain_height already branches on to decide land or seabed, exposed on its own. It is positive on land, negative at sea, and zero exactly at the coastline, which makes it followable: its gradient points at the nearest coast. A boolean land test could not do this. It is flat on both sides and tells a searcher nothing about which way to go.

A soft ditch and a hard one score the same. Grading them would hand the policy a gradient pointing at a tidier way to get it wrong. Shaping is potential-based, so by Ng, Harada & Russell (1999) it cannot change which policy is optimal — only how fast it is found.

Observations 15–17 are deliberately in the ship's frame — the same frame command_thrust is expressed in — so the policy can map "land is that way" onto a translation without first having to learn the rotation between two coordinate systems.

Where the spawn distribution is doing the work

Episodes start over dry land 75% of the time. Not always, or the policy never sees water and has no reason to learn the difference; not uniformly, because roughly 60% of these planets is ocean and a ship dropped mid-ocean cannot reach any coast inside the step limit however good it is. This is an honest limit rather than a tuned constant: the land signal is a local gradient, so an agent walks to the nearest coast and cannot know a better one lies behind it. Where the field goes flat, far out to sea, the signal is zero and the correct behaviour is to hover until the clock runs out. The task is "do not come down in the sea", not "cross one".

Rig

Two processes in lock-step

Godot owns the physics; PyTorch owns the gradients. They exchange one line of JSON per physics tick over loopback. The read blocks inside _physics_process, which stalls the whole engine until Python answers — that is the point. Nothing is dropped, nothing races, and a rollout replays exactly.

The agent writes to Ship.command_thrust and Ship.command_turn — the same seam a mouse and keyboard feed. Everything downstream is the shipping code. That was a deliberate constraint: a policy trained against a simplified copy of the ship is a policy that cannot fly the real one, and you find that out last.

Watching it happen

Passing --render opens a window on the run instead of flying blind: a chase camera that follows whichever agent is nearest the ground, the trainer's own numbers pushed across the same socket, and a bar per ship in the batch.

Speed is playback, not physics: the step stays 1/60 of a simulated second, so a rendered run and a headless one from the same seed produce identical trajectories — verified by the smoke test returning the same figures to the decimal. It costs throughput and nothing else, about 2,700 steps/s against 7,241. The batch strip is what earns the window: a policy that has learned to land and one that has learned to hover look the same through a chase camera for ten seconds, and completely different as sixteen altitude bars that stop going down.

Log

Eleven things that were wrong

In order, because each was only visible once the previous had been fixed. Seven are reward or action-space design, two are the simulation itself, one is ordinary engineering — and the last is the one that invalidated the others' scoreboard.

  1. Shaping scaled by the wrong speed

    Observations are normalised by orbital speed at the surface — about 42 units/s. Reusing that scale in the reward made the entire difference between a 6 units/s crash and a perfect touchdown worth about 0.7 reward, against a 100-point landing bonus. The policy correctly ignored speed.

    Rescaling to a landing-relevant 10 units/s was the first change that moved the landing rate at all. A normalisation constant that is right for the network's input can be badly wrong for the objective.

  2. A pass/fail bonus is a cliff

    The first reward paid +100 for a clean touchdown and nothing otherwise. Every near miss scored identically to a forty-units-per-second smash, so there was no gradient pointing back toward a landing — only a plateau with a hole in it.

    Scoring speed and alignment continuously turned "not quite a landing" into a slope. The strict pass/fail test survives, but only to label outcomes in the logs.

  3. Penalising crashes taught it to refuse to land

    With an impact-scaled penalty, hovering for a full episode cost about −1.8 while a bad landing cost −3. The policy learned to stall just off the deck until the clock ran out — which was, given that reward, the correct answer.

    timeout rate: 24.9% -> 40.6% -> 80.0% -> 87.5% (iterations 20-60)

    The penalty is now flat and small, and time is charged at a rate that makes running out the clock worse than any touchdown at all.

  4. Alignment was gated but barely shaped

    Belly-down alignment gates the terminal bonus outright. At a shaping weight of 0.3 it was worth at most 3 reward across a whole episode, against 120 for landing. The policy learned to arrive fast and roughly sideways.

    Raising the weight to 1.5 roughly doubled the landing rate inside 400k steps. Shape a gate in proportion to the gate.

  5. Thrust was direction-only, so noise replaced the command

    Ship._physics_process thrusts along movement.normalized(). A keyboard has no analogue axis, so the player's thrust was always all-or-nothing — fine for a person. For a policy exploring with Gaussian noise it is poison: when the mean command is near zero, the noise does not perturb the direction, it is the direction, and the ship fires at full power in a random one.

    The first fix was a deadzone, so "no thrust" could be expressed at all. That created the next problem.

  6. The deadzone made the mean policy a different policy

    Training samples actions; play takes the distribution mean. With a deadzone, a mean action can sit inside the dead region while sampled actions routinely escape it — so the policy learned to rely on its own exploration noise to produce thrust at all, and taking the mean removed the thrust entirely.

    Replacing the discontinuity with continuous thrust authority — command length scales the force — made the mean representative again. This is the bug I would most expect to ship unnoticed: every training metric looked healthy throughout.

  7. A cloned actor with a random critic

    Behaviour cloning arrives with a competent actor and an untrained critic. Its advantage estimates are noise, and the first PPO updates spend that noise dismantling the policy.

    Iterations of value-only updates before the policy is allowed to move made the fine-tune stop being an immediate demolition. It still has not made it an improvement — see the curves above.

  8. The autopilot waited for an altitude the ship cannot reach

    The training environment ends an episode at altitude zero, measured to the ship's origin, because there are no colliders in training. In the game the hull has landing legs, so a ship resting on the ground reads about 1.4.

    The in-game autopilot waited for zero. It flew the descent perfectly, eight for eight, and then hovered a metre and a half off the deck until it timed out. No test living inside the training environment could have caught this.

    before: 8/8 "disengaged (timed out) at altitude 1.4-1.9" after: 8/8 "landed speed 1.35-1.42 align 1.000"

  9. The planets were noise, not terrain

    continent_frequency means features per planet. Two of three were set to 100.0, which measured 50.9° and 39.5° of mean ground slope — a bed of nails rather than a world, and nothing a ship with landing struts can sit on. Back near 1.0 the same noise makes continents and the slope drops to 5–7°.

    Worth writing the measuring tool for. "Too spiky" is an impression; 50.9° over an eight-unit baseline is a fact, and it also says which parameter is responsible.

  10. The ship could not stop

    Thrust 10.0 against gravity 9.81 is a thrust-to-weight ratio of 1.02, and flight assist deliberately stops damping whatever axis you are thrusting along. A ship braking its own descent therefore got 0.19 units/s² and nothing else — 31 seconds and ninety units of altitude to shed a six-unit fall, from a spawn that is never that high.

    Two subsystems, each defensible alone, that together removed the ship's only way to stop. Thrust is now 18.0. Then the controller needed retuning too — its gain of 15 was sized for authority that had not existed, and left most of the new thrust unused until it went to 45.

    TWR 1.02, gain 15 0.0% landed 3.11 u/s (after thrust fix, before retune) TWR 1.83, gain 45 99.3% landed 1.04 u/s

  11. And the scoreboard had been measuring splashdowns all along

    Everything above was tuned against a landing rate that counted ocean impacts as landings, because the planets were 90–97% ocean, water brakes harder than the flight assist, and the success test only asked whether the ship arrived gently — never where.

    The fixes above are all still real, and the lessons in them survive. But every number they were justified by had to be remeasured, which is the expensive way to learn that a metric deserves the same scrutiny as the thing it measures. See the correction at the top.

The diagnosis

This is a different failure from the one this page used to describe. Previously the return climbed while landings fell, and the reward was to blame. Here both fall together — 75 to 21, and 40% to 2.5% — so the policy is not exploiting the objective, it is simply getting worse at it. That points at the fine-tuning setup: a critic that begins ignorant of a policy already near optimum, and exploration noise wide enough to leave the basin the clone was sitting in. Cloning found a good policy and policy gradient walked out of it.

The dashed line is where the clone actually sits when evaluated at the distribution mean, which is how it is flown in game. The gap between it and the sampled curve at iteration one is the exploration noise, and it is worth noticing that the gap alone is most of the difference between a shipped policy and a useless one.

Ship

It runs in the game

The network is not a demo sitting beside the project. Get in the ship, get near a planet, press P. rl/neural_pilot.gd loads policy.json and runs the forward pass in GDScript — four small matrix-vector products, no ONNX runtime, no GDExtension, nothing to install.

Three guards make that claim checkable rather than hopeful. The observation has exactly one definition, LandingEnv.build_observation, used by both the trainer and the cockpit; if they drifted the policy would still run, still look busy, and quietly fly a different task. The GDScript forward pass is diffed against the numpy one on fixed probes. And the end-to-end check flies main.tscn itself.

forward pass largest disagreement 4.67e-07 (tolerance 2e-04) PASS autoland_check 8/8 landed on verified dry ground speed 1.35-1.42 u/s align 1.000

That last check deliberately does not go through LandingEnv. It drives the real Ship node with real terrain colliders and the ship's own auto-levelling, and it now scores land versus water itself — because a check that inherited the environment's blind spot would have reported 8/8 through the entire splashdown era.

Next

What I would change

The environment, the bridge and the deployment path are solid. The fine-tune is not, and the evaluation harness has just demonstrated how much rests on it.

  • Fix the fine-tune before touching the reward. Regress the critic on Monte-Carlo returns from the demonstration rollout rather than freezing the actor and hoping, and put a floor under the policy standard deviation so the sampled and mean policies cannot drift apart unreported.
  • Score alignment against the terrain normal, not the radial. "Belly toward the planet centre" is the wrong target on a slope, and Planet3 still has a 99th-percentile slope of 27°.
  • Give the land signal some range. A single-point gradient finds the nearest coast, not the best one. Sampling a ring at a couple of angular radii would let an agent choose a landing site rather than merely walk uphill.
  • Terminate on the collider, not on altitude zero. The agent has still never touched a collision mesh; the ship rests on its struts about 1.4 units above the analytic surface, and that gap is invisible to every test living inside training.
  • Assert on the outcome distribution, not just the rate. The splashdown bug would have been caught on day one by a smoke test that asserted episodes end somewhere plausible. A single number cannot report that it is measuring the wrong thing; a distribution usually can.

The larger lesson is the ordinary one, and it is worth more than a working policy would have been. On a task where a competent controller fits in ten lines, reinforcement learning has to earn its place, and here it still has not. What made that legible was keeping a hand-written baseline in the evaluation harness from the start — and what nearly hid it for good was trusting a success metric that had never been asked to defend itself.
